# HUMAN HISTORY
## A Multidisciplinary Reconstruction of Humanity's Past

**Project Type:** Open Historical Research, Archaeological Investigation, Genealogical Analysis, and Civilizational Reconstruction  
**Project Name:** Human History  
**Core Mission:** Reconstruct the most comprehensive, evidence-based account of humanity's origins, development, migrations, civilizations, technologies, beliefs, and possible lost chapters.

---

# 1. PROJECT MISSION

Build an extensive, interconnected research platform dedicated to investigating the complete history of humanity.

The objective is to examine the totality of available evidence to develop the clearest possible understanding of:

- Where humanity originated.
- How humans spread across the planet.
- How populations mixed, migrated, and developed.
- How the earliest civilizations emerged.
- Whether sophisticated societies existed before currently recognized civilizations.
- How ancient knowledge, architecture, astronomy, mathematics, and engineering developed.
- What ancient people believed about their origins and interactions with the world.
- Whether major historical events have been misunderstood, misdated, or incompletely documented.
- Whether unexplained archaeological, genetic, astronomical, or historical evidence points toward currently unresolved chapters of human history.

Do not constrain the investigation to conventional historical narratives.

Examine mainstream academic interpretations, alternative hypotheses, ancient religious traditions, oral histories, unconventional archaeological interpretations, and unexplained discoveries.

However, do not replace conventional assumptions with alternative assumptions. Both must withstand evidence-based scrutiny.

**The ultimate objective is not to tell a predetermined story. It is to discover the strongest possible reconstruction of what actually happened.**

# 2. CORE RESEARCH PRINCIPLES

## 2.1 Evidence Before Conclusions

Every major historical claim must be evaluated against available evidence.

Never accept a claim simply because:
- It appears in a textbook.
- It is widely repeated.
- An academic institution endorses it.
- An ancient religious text describes it.
- A popular alternative historian promotes it.
- It fits an appealing narrative.

Every claim must be traceable to its supporting evidence.

## 2.2 Intellectual Independence

Investigate subjects without automatically dismissing them because they are unconventional.

Areas deserving serious investigation include:

- Ancient advanced civilizations.
- Lost cities and submerged settlements.
- Megalithic construction.
- Ancient astronomical knowledge.
- Prehistoric seafaring.
- Unexplained technological artifacts.
- Ancient accounts of beings descending from the sky.
- UFO and UAP reports throughout history.
- Possible interactions between geographically separated ancient populations.
- Catastrophic events and civilization collapses.
- Unusual genetic ancestry and population movements.
- Ancient traditions describing earlier ages of humanity.

These subjects should receive the same fundamental treatment as conventional historical questions: investigate the evidence, identify competing explanations, and determine what can and cannot be established.

## 2.3 Separate Evidence from Interpretation

Every finding must be assigned an evidence classification:

| Classification | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Established | Supported by multiple independent, high-quality evidence sources |
| Strongly Supported | Substantial evidence, with some unresolved questions |
| Probable | Best-supported interpretation, but not conclusive |
| Plausible | Consistent with available evidence, but insufficiently demonstrated |
| Speculative | Possible, but currently supported by limited evidence |
| Disputed | Competing interpretations exist |
| Unsupported | No adequate evidence currently establishes the claim |
| Contradicted | Available evidence conflicts with the claim |

Never present speculation as established history.

Equally, never describe something as impossible merely because it has not yet been explained.

## 2.4 Preserve Uncertainty

Historical evidence is incomplete.

Archaeological sites are destroyed. Coastal settlements disappear beneath rising seas. Written records are lost. Genetic information becomes degraded. Ancient oral traditions change over generations.

The project must explicitly represent uncertainty, missing information, and competing interpretations.

Acknowledge when an answer is genuinely unknown.

## 3.2 Human Genetics and Genealogy

Build a comprehensive understanding of human population history using genetics.

Investigate:

- Mitochondrial DNA.
- Y-chromosome ancestry.
- Autosomal DNA.
- Ancient DNA.
- Haplogroups.
- Genetic admixture.
- Population bottlenecks.
- Founder effects.

- Ancient population replacement.
- Gene flow between continents.
- Archaic human ancestry.
- Genetic relationships between ancient and modern populations.

Explore ancient population movements and compare genetic evidence against archaeological and linguistic models.

Important research questions:

- What can mitochondrial and Y-chromosome lineages actually establish?
- How reliable are proposed migration dates?
- Which populations experienced major genetic replacement?
- Which populations preserve unusually deep regional ancestry?
- How much Neanderthal and Denisovan ancestry survives in modern populations?
- Can ancient DNA reveal migrations that are invisible in archaeological records?
- Where do genetic models disagree with conventional historical narratives?

**Important:** Do not confuse genetic ancestry with cultural identity, language, nationality, or an unbroken genealogical family tree.

Individual genealogical investigations must distinguish documented family relationships from population-level genetic inference. Protect the privacy of living individuals.

- Genetic samples from excavated burials.
- Ancient climate conditions.
- Local geological formations.

- Possible trade routes.

# 12. COMPETING HISTORICAL HYPOTHESES

Build a formal system for maintaining multiple explanations of historical events.

For example:

**Question:** How did the ancient pyramids develop?

Possible explanations:
- Development from earlier Egyptian mastaba and step-pyramid traditions.
- Gradual refinement of stone construction.
- Multiple generations of architectural experimentation.
- Lost or incompletely understood construction methods.
- External cultural influence.
- Other hypotheses supported by future discoveries.

Each hypothesis must include:

1. Description.
2. Supporting evidence.
3. Contradicting evidence.
4. Required assumptions.
5. Testable predictions.
6. Known weaknesses.
7. Confidence assessment.
8. What new discovery would strengthen or falsify it.

The system should never force one explanation to be selected when the evidence remains inconclusive.

Allow hypotheses to be updated as new research becomes available.

# 14. RESEARCH WORKFLOW

Every major research investigation should follow a consistent process.

### Phase 1: Establish the Question

Define exactly what historical question is being investigated.

### Phase 2: Collect Evidence

Gather archaeological, genetic, geological, textual, linguistic, and historical evidence.

### Phase 3: Establish Chronology

Determine when the evidence originated and how securely it can be dated.

### Phase 4: Evaluate Provenance

Determine where the evidence came from and whether its context is reliable.

### Phase 5: Compare Interpretations

Identify mainstream, alternative, and unresolved explanations.

### Phase 6: Cross-Disciplinary Analysis

Compare findings from independent research disciplines.

### Phase 7: Test Hypotheses

Identify what each explanation predicts and whether available evidence supports those predictions.

### Phase 8: Document Uncertainty

Record what is known, unknown, disputed, and speculative.

### Phase 9: Update the Historical Model

Integrate findings into the global timeline, geographic database, and evidence graph.

### Phase 10: Publish Findings

Produce an accessible research report that explains the evidence and conclusions.

13. Build the map and timeline as foundational components.
14. Make the research database expandable.
15. Keep the system modular and suitable for years of additional research.

When investigating a topic, do not stop after finding the first explanation.

Look for contradictory evidence, alternative interpretations, missing records, and unresolved questions.

When an accepted explanation is strong, explain why.

When an alternative explanation is weak, explain why.

When neither explanation is sufficient, preserve the question as unresolved.

# 20. FINAL OBJECTIVE

The finished Human History project should become an interactive, continually expanding reconstruction of humanity's past.

It should allow a researcher to move from the earliest evidence of human existence to the modern world, exploring:

- Our biological origins.
- Our genetic ancestry.
- Our migrations.
- Our earliest settlements.
- Our ancient civilizations.
- Our monumental structures.
- Our technologies.
- Our religions and traditions.
- Our environmental history.
- Our wars and collapses.
- Our unexplained discoveries.
- Our possible lost chapters.

The ultimate product is not simply a timeline of kings, wars, and civilizations.

It is a comprehensive model of humanity as a species, its development across the planet, and the accumulated evidence of everything that may have happened along the way.

**The guiding question is simple:**

*What actually happened to humanity, how did we get here, and what parts of our history have we not yet discovered or correctly understood?*

Follow the evidence. Challenge assumptions. Preserve uncertainty. Keep investigating.
