## Ask
What textual and material evidence shows alchemy / early chemistry in Greco-Roman Egypt (Alexandria, Panopolis): Leiden papyrus X, Stockholm papyrus, Pseudo-Democritus Physika kai Mystika, Zosimus of Panopolis, Mary the Jewess (Maria Prophetissa) and her apparatus, Cleopatra the Alchemist? Dates, languages, and what they actually describe (practical dye/metal recipes versus theory).

Is there any evidence for alchemy in pharaonic Egypt before the Ptolemies? State what is absent as well as what is present.

## Cited findings

### Leiden papyrus X and Stockholm papyrus (practical recipes)
- Leiden X is a Greek papyrus found at Thebes: 101 recipes plus 10 Dioscorides extracts (= 111), dated by its first editors Reuvens and Leemans to the end of the 3rd century CE; Berthelot calls it the oldest manuscript then known on such subjects. [Berthelot 1887](https://archive.org/details/collectiondesanc01bert)
- Berthelot reads it as the notebook of an artisan ("faussaire") practising goldsmithing and the dyeing of precious cloth; it covers precious-metal objects, not tools or arms. [Berthelot 1887](https://archive.org/details/collectiondesanc01bert)
- Stockholm papyrus (P. Holm.): "eine Sammlung chemischer Recepte", which cannot be the work of one person but reflects "die Erfahrung bezw. Spekulation mehrerer Generationen"; Lagercrantz notes the Theban origin is attested only for Leiden X and is a conjecture for Holm. [Lagercrantz 1913](https://archive.org/details/papyrusgraecusho00lage)
- Both papyri: "two anonymous recipe books on papyrus codices written in third- or fourth-century Egypt", over 200 recipes (softening/hardening metals, gold- and silver-coloured alloys, inks, fake gems, purple wool), linked to workshop practice. [Martelli 2020](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/3F2A7926F9D784A43E5170E0BA50AFB5/S2058850X20000041a.pdf/ancient-handbooks-and-graeco-egyptian-collections-of-alchemical-recipes.pdf) (DOI 10.1017/bjt.2020.4). Werrett dates them 250-300 CE, found in the Thebaid, "roughly contemporaneous with Zosimos". [Werrett 2024](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:45fed8a7-0777-4822-b4a2-0fcc788d0b64/files/dtm70mv88b)
- Leiden X is "exclusively practical" and lacks alchemical or philosophical elements. [Wikipedia, re-quoted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leyden_papyrus_X) The Leiden author even says a recipe will fool the craftsmen, i.e. it makes a product that looks like asem, not asem itself (Werrett, citing the Greek). [Werrett 2024](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:45fed8a7-0777-4822-b4a2-0fcc788d0b64/files/dtm70mv88b)

### Pseudo-Democritus, Physika kai Mystika
- Three unconnected parts: purple recipes of "rein technischen, also nicht alchimistischen Charakter"; a necromantic scene; alchemical gold and silver (chrysopoiia / argyropoiia) recipes. The real Democritus "mit der Schrift nichts zu thun gehabt"; no fixed date ("fehlen alle Stützpunkte"). [Lagercrantz 1913](https://archive.org/details/papyrusgraecusho00lage)
- Berthelot: the recipes are practical and "comparables de tous points" to Leiden X, but each ends with the refrain "la nature triomphe de la nature"; he places a Democritean school (Bolus of Mendes) near the end of the Ptolemies. [Berthelot 1887](https://archive.org/details/collectiondesanc01bert)
- Werrett: the text is "very poorly preserved", the earliest alchemical source that later Graeco-Egyptian authors cite by name, with the semi-mythical authors dated 1st to 3rd century; Ps.-Democritus is "often identified as Bolos of Mendes, though cf. Hershbell 1987". [Werrett 2024](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:45fed8a7-0777-4822-b4a2-0fcc788d0b64/files/dtm70mv88b)
- Synesius and Syncellus (quoted by Lagercrantz) have Democritus initiated by Ostanes in the temple at Memphis, with Maria and Pammenes present: a legend that puts the craft in Egyptian temples but is a late tradition, and Lagercrantz sees the Ostanes layer as 4th-century mystical reworking. [Lagercrantz 1913](https://archive.org/details/papyrusgraecusho00lage)

### Zosimus of Panopolis
- Zosimus writes c. 300 CE (Werrett: Panopolis, Akhmim; paganism strong into the 5th century; Gnostic and Hermetic milieu). He mixes apparatus and recipes with visionary and Hermetic allegory. [Werrett 2024](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:45fed8a7-0777-4822-b4a2-0fcc788d0b64/files/dtm70mv88b)
- Three modern views of his job: client scholar (Dufault 2019), temple metallurgist-priest (Grimes 2018), high-ranking member of a temple workshop (Escolano-Poveda 2022); Werrett favours the third, citing the Dendera Chamber of Gold for uninitiated specialists admitted to the sanctum. These are hypotheses; he adds "no direct evidence that Zosimos visited Dendera". [Werrett 2024](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:45fed8a7-0777-4822-b4a2-0fcc788d0b64/files/dtm70mv88b)
- Primary: in his Final Account, "Cet art divin a été révélé aux prêtres seuls"; elsewhere "Il y a deux sciences et deux sagesses : celle des Égyptiens et celle des Hébreux" (Livre véritable de Sophé). [Berthelot-Ruelle 1888](https://archive.org/details/collectiondesanc23bert)

### Mary the Jewess and Cleopatra
- Mary's Greek works survive only as quoted by Zosimus and later authors; Zosimus says "Marie, la première" described copper burned with sulphur and treated to become gold. Her sayings: "Joignez le mâle et la femelle...". [Berthelot-Ruelle 1888](https://archive.org/details/collectiondesanc23bert)
- Apparatus: the kerotakis, dibikos, tribikos are credited to Maria; Werrett: "not certain whether Maria invented, improved upon, or simply championed the kerotakis method"; Zosimus calls her authority on the "Jewish" tradition. [Werrett 2024](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:45fed8a7-0777-4822-b4a2-0fcc788d0b64/files/dtm70mv88b)
- Berthelot lists Cleopatra (distillation treatises with figures) and Marie (digestion apparatus, furnaces) among authors summarised in a compilation made "vers le IIIe siècle". The Cleopatra figures survive in Byzantine MSS; Cleopatra is a pseudonym (not re-confirmed this round beyond Wikipedia, which I opened but did not keep). [Berthelot 1887](https://archive.org/details/collectiondesanc01bert)
- Berthelot also reports a story that Diocletian burned Egyptian gold-and-silver books c. 290 (quoted from older sources; not independently checked).

### Pharaonic Egypt: what is present and absent
- Present: the craft base. Berthelot (1887) credits "l'industrie des orfèvres et métallurgistes égyptiens"; Werrett notes Egyptian temples were centres of metallurgical production, citing the Dendera Chamber of Gold; Grimes (via Werrett) cites a Syriac Zosimus fragment on ritual metallurgy for cult statues. [Berthelot 1887](https://archive.org/details/collectiondesanc01bert), [Werrett 2024](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:45fed8a7-0777-4822-b4a2-0fcc788d0b64/files/dtm70mv88b)
- Present only as claim: Zosimus asserts a priestly origin; Werrett reports Zosimus believed in a "singular primordial alchemical tradition" transmitted "from Old Kingdom Egypt to the Late Antique period". That is Zosimus's belief, reported, not tested. [Werrett 2024](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:45fed8a7-0777-4822-b4a2-0fcc788d0b64/files/dtm70mv88b)
- Absent in everything I opened: any pharaonic-period or Ptolemaic text that describes transmutation, tinctures, or imitation-gold recipes. Every dated alchemical document found is of the 3rd century CE or later (Leiden/Stockholm 250-300 CE; Zosimus c. 300; Cleopatra's figures in 10th-11th century copies). Wikipedia: Zosimus's pharaonic claim has "little to no evidence" (tertiary; support not traced).
- Grimes's definition (as quoted by Werrett): early Greco-Egyptian alchemy is "first through fourth centuries CE", rooted in "artisanal traditions involving the coloration of metals".

## Sceptical and alternative pass
- Scepticism within the texts: Lagercrantz denies Democritus's authorship and calls the "Anaxilaus" attribution fraud-adjacent; Berthelot calls the Leiden owner a forger; the Leiden recipe itself boasts of deceiving craftsmen. These show a sceptical reading: much of what the papyri teach is imitation of gold and silver, not transmutation.
- Against an early (pharaonic) date: nothing in Lagercrantz, Berthelot, Martelli or Werrett provides a text earlier than the Roman period. Berthelot's own reasoning from craft to alchemy is an inference.
- Alternative claims: Zosimus's priestly-origin claim; Grimes/Escolano-Poveda's temple-workshop reading (via Werrett); Berthelot's Egyptian-goldsmith origin. None shows pharaonic "alchemy" itself.
- Tension noted: Martelli dates the papyri "third- or fourth-century", Werrett 250-300, Berthelot end of 3rd century. Wikipedia pages vary on Zosimus and Mary dates (c. 300 vs 4th century); Mary is placed 1st-3rd century by Zosimus's comments (Wikipedia, not kept).
- Round 1's claim of "111 recipes" is consistent with Berthelot's 101+10.
