# khem-name-chemistry-etymology (round 2): Origin of 'chemistry'/'alchemy' and Kemet

## Ask
1. Evidence and scholarly status of the claim that 'chemistry'/'alchemy' derives from Kemet/Khem/'black land' (kmt); competing etymologies (Greek chyma/cheo, Arabic kimiya, others); who argues which; which is most widely accepted and why.
2. What do the earliest attestations of chemeia/chymeia say (Zosimus, Suda, Plutarch's Chemia for Egypt)?

## Cited findings
- Plutarch, De Iside 33 (opened in the Loeb scan, Greek and Babbitt's English): Egypt, "which has the blackest of soils", is called Chemia like the black of the eye and compared to a heart. Greek: "Χημίαν καλοῦσι καὶ καρδίᾳ παρεικάζουσι". This is an attestation of Egypt = Chemia (c. AD 100), about the country and its soil. It says nothing about metals or transmutation. — [Plutarch, Isis and Osiris, Loeb](https://archive.org/details/plutarch-isis-osiris-loeb)
- Lindsay (1970) treats both theories. For Greek: chymia from cheein 'pour', chyma 'ingot' (3rd-c. BC Oropos, 2nd-c. BC Delos inscriptions), so the name "intrudes from the metallurgical world". For Egyptian: he judges the 'Black Art' reading philologically weak ("not much to be said"), but allows an echo of Khime in the minds of practitioners; and rejects a kmj.f (Book of Wisdom) origin as "highly farfetched". He says the earlier form cannot be proved. — [Lindsay](https://archive.org/details/alchemy-jack-lindsay)
- Lindsay also gives the Diocletian story via Panodoros/Syncellus: Diocletian "had burned about 290 of the ancient books of Chemia dealing with gold and silver". His notes cite the Suda s.vv. Diocletian and chemeia, but I did not open the Suda. — same source
- Wiktionary (χυμεία): the Zosimos tradition of an inventor Chymes, but "more probably" from χύμα + -εία; the Arabic and English forms descend through Late Greek (alchemy entry: Greek χυμεία from χύμα 'ingot, bar'). — [Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/χυμεία)
- Regal's sceptical encyclopedia gives the standard line: chemeia from smelting, Arabic al-kimiya, then English; it cites Newman & Principe 1998. — [Regal](https://archive.org/details/Pseudoscience_A_Critical_Encyclopedia_by_Brian_Regal)
- Wikipedia (finding aids): Budge/kmt vs Greek khuma/khéō, Mahn's folk-etymology caution, Diels 1914 for chyma, and Zosimos's 'chema' book via Syncellus. — [Etymology of chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etymology_of_chemistry), [Alchemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alchemy), [Zosimos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zosimos_of_Panopolis)
- Status (my reading of what I opened, not a stated consensus): Arabic al-kīmiyā from Late Greek chēmeia/chymeia is uncontested. For the Greek word's own origin, the Greek chyma ('cast metal') derivation is the one the dictionary and the reference work give, and the one Lindsay finds philologically stronger; the Egyptian kmt link is an old, still-cited alternative, probably also a folk association made by the ancients. No source I opened proves either.

## Sceptical and alternative pass
Sceptical: Lindsay (philology weak), Wiktionary ('more probably' χύμα), Regal, Mahn via Wikipedia. Alternative: Budge's kmt/kēme theory (via Wikipedia only) and Lindsay's report of a kmj.f theory (rejected). Zosimos's own 'Chymes/chema' tale is an ancient etymology of the same kind. Popular blogs (not candidates) assert an Egyptian origin; none opened as evidence.
