# inscriptions-and-science-primary: Primary inscriptions and texts for writing and science transmission

## Ask
"Quote the primary inscriptions and texts for writing and science transmission: the Ahiram sarcophagus inscription (Phoenician, c. 1000 BCE); the Dipylon oinochoe and Nestor's Cup from Pithekoussai (earliest Greek alphabetic inscriptions, c. 740-720 BCE); an Etruscan abecedarium (Marsiliana tablet); Ptolemy's Almagest (Babylonian eclipse records used by Hipparchus, Almagest IV.2 and V.14); al-Khwarizmi's Algebra (Rosen 1831 translation, opening); the Fihrist of Ibn al-Nadim on the translation movement; Robert of Chester's preface to the Latin Algebra or the Liber de compositione alchemiae."
"Give composition or inscription date with basis, the object or manuscript witness, and the edition read."

Output: 20 candidates in `inscriptions-and-science-primary.candidates.json` (13 with a `text` object, 14 with a `strand`). The `verified` marker is absent by design (the verifier adds it). Each quote was run through `check_quote.py` before recording (see "Verification method" for the three PDFs).

## Cited findings

### Writing: Phoenician to Greek to Etruscan
- Ahiram sarcophagus (Byblos): the 38-word Phoenician text names the maker, a son of Ahiram, and the dead king ("for Aḥirom, his father, as he laid him down for eternity"). The page calls it the oldest Phoenician-alphabet witness of considerable length. Date disputed: "around 1000 BCE" (Cook 1994, cited) versus c. 850 BCE or the 9th-8th century (Porada, Wallenfels, cited). I recorded -1000 to -850. — [Wikipedia: Ahiram sarcophagus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahiram_sarcophagus)
- Dipylon oinochoe: Late Geometric Attic jug, hexameter "Whoever of all these dancers now plays most delicately," c. 740 BC, found 1871, NAMA 192. — [Wikipedia: Dipylon inscription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipylon_inscription)
- Nestor's Cup (Pithekoussai): "I am the cup of Nestor good for drinking. Whoever drinks from this cup, desire for beautifully crowned Aphrodite will seize him instantly." Euboean alphabet, c. 735-720 BC, Tomb 168, San Montano, found 1954; the first line has a lacuna with at least fifteen proposed restorations. — [Wikipedia: Nestor's Cup (Pithekoussai)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nestor%27s_Cup_(Pithekoussai))
- Marsiliana tablet: Mnamon (Scuola Normale Superiore) says it "is thus at the same time both the oldest Etruscan and the oldest Greek alphabetary" and dates the Greek alphabet in Etruria to the 8th century BC; Wikipedia says about 700 BC, 26 letters, and an adaptation of the Euboean alphabet of Pithekoussai and Cumae. — [Mnamon](https://mnamon.sns.it/index.php?page=Esempi&id=10&lang=en); [Wikipedia: Etruscan alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etruscan_alphabet)
- Herodotus 5.58 (Godley): Phoenicians with Cadmus "brought with them to Hellas ... the alphabet"; Ionians learned it from them. Greek narrative tradition, route stated not proved. — [Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0126%3Abook%3D5%3Achapter%3D58)
- Fihrist (Dodge 1970): "bringing sixteen letters with which the Greeks wrote" (Cadmus and a second man from Egypt), a tenth-century Arabic echo of the tradition, with a different origin from Herodotus. — [Dodge, archive.org](https://archive.org/download/fihrist-of-al-nadim.-a-tenth-century-survey-of-muslim-culture.-ed.-and-transl.-b/Fihrist%20of%20al-Nadim.%20A%20Tenth-Century%20Survey%20of%20Muslim%20Culture.%20Ed.%20and%20transl.%20by%20B.%20Dodge.%20Vol.%201-2.%201970_djvu.txt)
- Date of the Greek adoption is contested: "Naveh (1973) for the 11th century BC, Stieglitz (1981) for the 14th century", Bernal earlier; "none ... widely accepted" per the page. — [Wikipedia: History of the Greek alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Greek_alphabet)

### Science: Babylon to Greece (Almagest), Greece to Arabic (Fihrist), Arabic to Latin (Algebra)
- Almagest IV.2 (Toomer): "Hipparchus already proved, by calculations from observations made by the Chaldaeans and in his time" that the old 6585-day "Periodic" was inexact; he found 126007 days plus 1 hour holding 4267 months. Toomer's footnote on the same page: "Ptolemy's account here is not historically accurate"; Hipparchus took his parameters from Babylonian sources and confirmed them with eclipses 345 years apart. — [Toomer scan](https://classicalliberalarts.com/resources/PTOLEMY_ALMAGEST_ENGLISH.pdf)
- Almagest V.14 (Toomer): the eclipse "in the fifth year of Nabopolassar" (-620 Apr. 21/22) is timed "at the end of the eleventh hour in Babylon". Another Babylonian triple is in IV.11 (Hipparchus, "brought over from Babylon"), not in the brief's loci but on the same scan. — same URL
- Jones 1991: "Hipparchus had access to Babylonian data, both observational and theoretical" on a much larger scale than any earlier Greek source; Kugler (1900) traced his lunar period relations to cuneiform System B. — [Jones, Isis 82 (NYU archive)](https://archive.nyu.edu/bitstream/2451/49537/2/Jones%201991%20Adaptation%20of%20Babylonian%20Methods%20Isis.pdf)
- Fihrist (Dodge 1970): al-Ma'mun's Aristotle dream "was one of the most definite reasons for the output of books"; he asked the Byzantine emperor for old scientific manuscripts; the Almagest section names the Arabic translators (Yahya ibn Khalid, al-Hajjaj ibn Matar, al-Nayrizi, Ishaq ibn Hunayn, Thabit). Fihrist composed 987-988 CE per Dodge's introduction. — same Dodge URL
- Rosen 1831, al-Khwarizmi's preface: al-Ma'mun's fondness for science "has encouraged me to compose a short work on Calculating by (the rules of) Completion and Reduction". Rosen's own introduction: "far more probable that the Arabs received their first knowledge of Algebra from the Hindus". Copy: Bodleian MS Hunt 214, A.H. 743 (A.D. 1342). — [Rosen, archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/algebraofmohamme00khuwuoft/algebraofmohamme00khuwuoft_djvu.txt)
- Karpinski 1915 (English of Robert of Chester): Latin version opens "Praise God the creator who has bestowed upon man the power to discover the significance of numbers"; colophon: Robert of Chester translated it at Segovia, Spanish Era 1183 (A.D. 1145). Karpinski says the Arabic introduction is absent from the extant Latin versions, and dates al-Khwarizmi's activity to about 825 (al-Mamun reigned 813-833). — [Karpinski, archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/robertofchesters00khuw/robertofchesters00khuw_djvu.txt)

## Sceptical and alternative pass
- Almagest's Babylonian records: Newton ("The Strange Case of Claudius Ptolemy", APL Technical Digest 16(2)) says "most of those early Babylonian 'observations' were in fact fabricated by Ptolemy" and tags the -620 and -522 eclipses of V.14 as fabricated. Against that, Toomer and Jones treat Babylonian records and parameters as real inputs to Hipparchus (Toomer corrects the route, not the fact). Recorded as a `skeptical` candidate with strand `debated`. A search snippet also mentioned a newly published cuneiform text confirming one Babylonian eclipse of Ptolemy; I did not open it, so it is only a lead.
- Date of the Greek alphabet: early-date proposals (Naveh, Stieglitz, Bernal) recorded from Wikipedia as `skeptical`; they are about timing, not about whether Greeks got letters from Phoenicians.
- Fihrist dream: Gutas (1998, ch. 3 "The Aristotelian dream and the ideology of rationalism") treats dreams as vehicles of propaganda and the dream story as to be read critically; recorded as `secondary`, stance `fails_to_support` for dream-as-cause. I read the opening of the section only.
- al-Khwarizmi's sources: Rosen (1831) argues for a Hindu route and against Diophantus as a source; modern Babylonian, Greek and Indian strand debates (a search surfaced Høyrup, Rashed, Cajori only through secondhand snippets) were not opened, so the strand is `debated` on Rosen alone and the post-1831 literature is a gap.
- One-sidedness: the inscriptions are not themselves contested texts (readings are), and for them the cautions are about date and reading (Ahiram 1000 vs 850 BCE; Nestor's lacuna; Dipylon fragmentary tail). Where a claimed relationship is stated (Herodotus, Fihrist Cadmus, Almagest/Babylon, Rosen/India), I quoted the passage and a dissent or caution as its own candidate; the Almagest and Greek-alphabet strands are well covered, the al-Khwarizmi and Robert of Chester strands have only 19th/early-20th-century editors.

## Verification method (read this before trusting the quotes)
- All Wikipedia, Mnamon, Perseus, archive.org (Rosen, Karpinski, Dodge, Gutas) and Jones quotes pass `check_quote.py <URL> "<quote>"` directly. Wikipedia pages twice returned an HTTP error to the script and passed on retry.
- Toomer's Almagest (26 MB scan, classicalliberalarts.com PDF): too large for the script's fetch timeout. I downloaded that URL, ran `pdftotext -layout`, and confirmed the three quotes on the local text with whitespace removed, because the OCR splits words ("m ade", "H ipparchus", "liis" for "his"). The IV.2 quote stops before the OCR error ("in liis time") so every word quoted is a correct word on the scan.
- Newton PDF (9 MB): `check_quote.py` uses `pdftotext -layout`, which interleaves the two columns, so it returns NOT FOUND. The quote was confirmed with plain `pdftotext` on the same downloaded URL.
- Quotes keep the page's own spelling (for example "Aḥirom", "Praise God the creator", "Calculating by (the rules of) Completion and Reduction").
- `validate_candidates.py` reports only the "no verifier marker" error for all 20 candidates (the verifier stage adds it); no other errors.
