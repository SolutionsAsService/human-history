# indianization-se-asia: Indianization of Southeast Asia (thread)

## Ask
- Southeast Asia: transmission of Sanskrit, Hindu-Buddhist religion and Indian scripts (Pallava-derived) to Funan, Champa, Java, Cambodia. Primary: Vo Canh inscription, Kutai Yupa inscriptions, Angkor-period Sanskrit inscriptions (Cœdès, Finot, Majumdar translations), Chinese envoy accounts (Kang Tai and Zhu Ying on Funan in the Liang shu or Nan Qi shu).
- Include sceptical or alternative scholarship on 'Indianization' (Wolters, Mabbett, Hall) and what is documented versus inferred.

18 candidates in `indianization-se-asia.candidates.json`; every quote was confirmed with `check_quote.py` against the URL cited (all 18 passed).

## Cited findings
- Vo Canh stele (Champa, Khanh Hoa): damaged Sanskrit text in vasantatilaka metre; a king of the line of Sri Mara dedicates his treasure to the welfare of creatures. Finot reads the ideas as Buddhist ("L'esprit du grand Açoka revit dans cet édit") and calls it the oldest inscription of Indochina. The kingdom is not named in the legible lines; no date opened (Finot defers to Bergaigne). — [Finot, BEFEO 15 (1915)](https://cdn.angkordatabase.asia/libs/docs/finot-notes-epigraphiques-befeo15-1915.pdf)
- Kutai Yupa inscriptions (Borneo): Sanskrit verse naming "the great Kundunga", his son Asvavarman, and Mulavarman, who sacrificed a Bahusuvarnaka sacrifice; post prepared by "the chief amongst the twice-borns"; a gift of cattle to Brahmins at Vaprakesvara; a post "made by the priests who had come hither". Vogel finds the script closest to archaic Pallava Grantha, accepts Kern's c. 400 CE and puts the order Bhadravarman (Champa), Mulavarman, Purnavarman. The date and script link are palaeographic inference, with no early Pallava stone records to compare. — [Vogel, BKI 74 (1918), archive.org scan](https://archive.org/stream/dli.ministry.10312/26759_djvu.txt)
- Funan, Chinese reports: Kang Tai's lost report survives in later quotations (Taiping yulan, Shuijing zhu) and in the Jin, Southern Qi and Liang histories. Hundian arrives by ship, shoots an arrow through Queen Liuye's boat. The sources differ on his home (Mofu, Ji, Jiao "to the south", or "a foreign country"). Suwu's mission to India in Fan Zhan's reign marks first documented Funan-India envoys. — [Fukami, "Hundian and Suwu"](https://stars.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/8115/files/KJ00006141347.pdf)
- Liang shu on Kaundinya (translated by Briggs): Indian Brahman, told by a spirit to reign in Funan, chosen king, "changed all the rules according to the customs of India"; Briggs dates him c. 400 CE. A Chinese report on Tun-hsiin cites over a thousand Indian Brahmans marrying locals; Tang histories say P'an-p'an and Lang-ya-hsiu learn Brahmanical writings. — [Briggs, The Khmer Empire and the Malay Peninsula](https://cdn.angkordatabase.asia/libs/docs/publications/the-khmer-empire-and-the-malay-peninsula/briggs-funan2.pdf)
- Funan-period Sanskrit inscriptions (Majumdar's summaries after Cœdès): Queen Kulaprabhavati's five-verse Visnu plaque at a Brahmin-inhabited town (king Jayavarman, c. 475-514), and the twelve-verse Prasat Pram Loven stele opening with an invocation to Buddha, naming Rudravarman and a Brahmin treasurer. — [Majumdar, Inscriptions of Kambuja (1953)](https://archive.org/stream/in.ernet.dli.2015.181497/2015.181497.Inscriptions-Of-Kambuja_djvu.txt)

## Sceptical and alternative pass
- Vickery (BEFEO 2003-04): Hun-t'ien's 1st-century date is too early ("no evidence of Indianization ... at that date"), the Funanese did not credit him with Indian culture, and Brahmin-borne Indian influence is "academic folklore". He treats the founding legend as oral tradition distinct from the envoys' own observations. — [Funan Reviewed](https://cdn.angkordatabase.asia/libs/docs/publications/funan-reviewed-deconstructing-the-ancients/funan-reviewed-vickery.pdf)
- Fukami: Hundian-from-India is "no more than a possibility"; the Liang shu puts his homeland south of Funan; Suwu's mission shows little earlier India contact, so Funan arose from Southeast Asian developments. Fukami still concedes Indian origin cannot be excluded. Note a date conflict: Fukami dates Kang Tai's mission 225-230 CE, Vickery 245-250 CE.
- Wolters' "localization" and Pollock's counter: Acri (2017) quotes Wolters' definition and reports Pollock's critique ("civilizationalist indigenism"; Sanskrit evidence carries Wolters' own case). — [Acri, Suvannabhumi 9(1)](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO201714052854214.pdf). Khu and Pranoto (2026) argue for multidirectional circulation but admit two of their cases are speculative. — [JSBA 11(3)](https://so04.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/JSBA/article/download/288981/193584/1336405)
- Documented vs inferred: Sanskrit inscriptions in Champa, Kutai and Funan are documented texts; dating rests on palaeography (Vogel) or on identification of kings (Cœdès via Majumdar). Brahmin agency, Pallava script origin and the Kaundinya story are inferred or debated; all strands are coded accordingly.
- One-sidedness: the sceptical side is well covered (Vickery, Fukami, Acri/Pollock, Khu). The Cœdès side is represented only through Majumdar and Briggs. Neither Wolters, Mabbett nor Hall could be opened in the original.
